\begin{abstract}
We study tabular Q-learning in a gridworld warehouse environment and ask whether training against an active adversary produces delivery policies that generalize better to out-of-distribution (OOD) layouts than policies trained only against stochastic obstacles. We frame the problem as a controlled comparison between a nature regime (stochastic forklift hazards) and an adversary regime (a deterministic or learned pursuer), evaluating both on a cross-scenario matrix under in-distribution and OOD layout conditions.

Our findings have four parts. First, training against a deterministic
pursuer when only a single training layout is available produces a robust and consistent OOD delivery advantage: $1.89\times$--$3.01\times$ 
more packages delivered across 16 seeded runs and two grid sizes, with no reversals. Second, a 
learned (strategic, co-evolving) adversary, once measured under valid state-space 
conditions, produces no OOD benefit. Where the deterministic pursuer yields $1.93\times$,
 the learned adversary yields $0.95\times$ in the first valid single-layout comparison. 
 These results are consistent with the idea that stable, predictable training pressure may be more useful for OOD transfer than a co-evolving opponent whose behavior changes during training. 
 Third, the key mechanism is state space coverage: multi-layout training more than 
 doubles OOD delivery for both adversary types, while state-space expansion beyond training coverage 
 hurts OOD performance. Fourth, the deterministic adversary effect has context-dependent
  boundary conditions under the multi-layout regime. Starting from a below-parity deterministic 
  baseline ($0.92\times$), some harder settings move the ratio above 1 
  (added packages: $1.81\times$; 7$\times$7 + 2 shelves: $1.26\times$), while others produce 
  only weak gains ($1.07\times$, $1.06\times$) or remain below parity 
  (2 agents: $0.89\times$, 62{,}500 states). Together, these results identify both the mechanism 
  and the conditions under which adversarial training improves robustness in warehouse navigation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Warehouse automation environments are highly dynamic and even chaotic in practice. Increasingly popular delivery robots must reach a package, pick it up, and deliver it while operating around traffic and disruptions (e.g., moving forklifts, shelving, blocked aisles). The central problem in this paper is not only whether an RL policy can solve one fixed environment, but whether a policy trained under one disturbance regime transfers robustly to unseen layouts and disturbance realizations.

Motivated by robust/adversarial RL and dynamic-warehouse navigation literature \cite{pinto2017rarl,tessler2019actionrobust,kristiansson2025warehouse}, we frame our work as a controlled comparison between two training strategies in the same gridworld task: (i) training against stochastic disturbances (``nature''), and (ii) training against an active adversary (``adversary''). We evaluate both trained policies under identical protocols with in-distribution evaluation (ID-A: same base layout as training) and out-of-distribution evaluation (OOD-layout: unseen layouts).

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A multi-seed demonstration that deterministic
        adversary training, when only a single training layout is available, produces
        consistent OOD delivery advantages
        ($1.89\times$--$3.01\times$) with no reversals across 16 seeded runs on 5$\times$5 and 7$\times$7 grids.
  \item A finding that reveals how state space explosion ($1{,}875
        \to 12.3\text{M}$ states) causes tabular Q-learning OOD evaluations to silently turn into random-walks, and a corrected measurement showing the
        learned adversary, under valid state-space conditions, produces a legitimate
        null result ($0.95\times$).
  \item A systematic study of state representation and training diversity showing
        that multi-layout training more than doubles OOD delivery for both adversary
        types, and that state space size is the bottleneck for OOD robustness in
        tabular Q-learning.
 \item Boundary condition experiments showing that under the multi-layout regime,
        deterministic adversary training can re-emerge as beneficial in some harder
        settings (added packages: $1.81\times$; 7$\times$7 + 2 shelves: $1.26\times$),
        produces only weak positive shifts in some others (added shelves on $5\times5$:
        $1.07\times$; added adversary: $1.06\times$), and remains below parity when
        cooperative multi-agent state space expansion (62{,}500 states) exceeds
        training coverage capacity ($0.89\times$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection*{Robust and Adversarial Reinforcement Learning}
A major challenge in reinforcement learning is that policies trained in one setting often perform poorly when the environment changes (different obstructions, dynamics, or disturbances). This can cause a gap between simulated and real-world performance. Pinto et al.~propose Robust Adversarial Reinforcement Learning (RARL), which frames robustness as a two-player, zero-sum minimax problem in which an adversary learns to apply destabilizing disturbances while the agent learns to succeed despite them \cite{pinto2017rarl}. Their results in continuous-control benchmarks demonstrate that explicitly training against a learned adversary can improve robustness and sometimes even performance in settings not trained on.

Tessler et al.~ also explore robustness with action uncertainty, where the agent's chosen action may be replaced by an adversarial alternative with some probability, or perturbed in continuous action spaces \cite{tessler2019actionrobust}. They relate these models to real-world robotics uncertainty (e.g., sudden pushes or actuator disturbances) and provide algorithms and evaluations in continuous-control domains.

\subsection*{Reinforcement Learning for Navigation in Dynamic Warehouses}
Warehouse navigation and path planning under changing conditions has also been studied from an applied perspective. Kristiansson and Winkelmann compare A* planning to Q-learning-based approaches for robot path planning in dynamic warehouse environments, evaluating performance primarily using throughput (packages delivered per robot over time) and showing that RL approaches can be more adaptable in highly dynamic scenarios (though it may be prone to overfitting) \cite{kristiansson2025warehouse}. This work supports the practical motivation for learning-based policies when obstacles and traffic patterns vary during operation.  

\subsection*{Multi-Agent and Moving-Opponent Settings}
Dynamic adversaries can also be viewed through a multi-agent lens. Paczolay et al.~study evasion with collision avoidance and propose a method combining Minimax-Q and Nash-Q to better account for opponents' actions in multi-agent settings \cite{paczolay2021pursuitevasion}. While the domain differs from warehouse logistics, it is a good example of modeling moving opponents and reasoning about adversarial behavior in grid-like environments.

\subsection*{Gap This Work Addresses}
Prior work either (i) applies adversarial RL to continuous domains without
gridworld logistics, (ii) studies dynamic warehouse navigation with stochastic
rather than strategic hazards, or (iii) models moving opponents in grid environments but in a capture rather than logistics setting. We combine all three: a logistics task in a gridworld with both stochastic and strategic disturbances, specifically asking whether strategic adversarial pressure transfers to OOD layout robustness.

\section{Task Definition and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Task Definition}

We model a warehouse as a discrete $N\times N$ gridworld. A single delivery agent
must navigate to a package location, pick it up, and deliver it to a designated
destination cell. The environment contains static shelves (blocked cells) and
dynamic hazards. An episode terminates on successful delivery, collision with a
terminal hazard, or timeout (200 steps on both 5$\times$5 and 7$\times$7).

At each step the agent selects one of four cardinal actions. The reward structure penalizes time and collisions while rewarding task progress (Figure~\ref{fig:rewards}). In the systematic evaluation reported later (Experiments~1--3 and 9--24), distance shaping (scale = 1.0) is applied uniformly to both nature and adversary policies. The exploratory runs in Section~\ref{sec:exploratory} do not all use shaping. Since both policies train under identical shaping in the systematic experiments, it does not bias the comparison. Delivery count is the primary task-success metric.

\subsection{Disturbance Regimes}

\textbf{Nature scenario.} A stochastic ``forklift'' agent moves with probability
0.5 at each step, choosing a random valid direction. The delivery agent must
navigate around it.

\textbf{Adversary scenario.} An active pursuer moves toward the delivery agent with probability 0.5.
We study two adversary designs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Deterministic pursuit: greedy Manhattan-distance minimization with
        random tie-breaking. Its policy is fixed.
  \item Learned adversary: a tabular Q-learner with a zero-sum objective
        against the delivery agent ($r_\text{adv} = -r_\text{delivery}$). It
        co-evolves with the delivery agent during training.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{State Representation}

For the base single-agent, single-package setting used in the main comparison
experiments, the agent's state encodes its position, the dynamic entity's
position, and the package status:
\[
  n_\text{states} = \underbrace{N^2}_{\text{agent pos}} \times \underbrace{N^2}_{\text{dynamic pos}} \times \underbrace{3}_{\text{pkg status}}
\]
For 5$\times$5 grids this gives $25 \times 25 \times 3 = 1{,}875$ states;
for 7$\times$7, $49 \times 49 \times 3 = 7{,}203$ states. Later boundary
experiments modify package count, agent count, and adversary count, which
changes the table size further. Section~\ref{sec:stateexp} describes experiments
with alternative encodings.


\subsection{Policy Comparison Protocol}

For each experimental run we train two delivery policies:
(1)~a nature-trained policy, and (2)~an adversary-trained policy.
Both are then evaluated in a cross-scenario matrix:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{N$\to$N}: nature-trained policy, nature evaluation
  \item \textbf{N$\to$A}: nature-trained policy, adversary evaluation
  \item \textbf{A$\to$N}: adversary-trained policy, nature evaluation
  \item \textbf{A$\to$A}: adversary-trained policy, adversary evaluation
\end{itemize}

Each matrix cell is evaluated under two layout conditions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ID-A}: the same layout(s) used during training
  \item \textbf{OOD-layout}: 10 or more unseen layouts with randomized shelf, package, and start positions, not used during training.
\end{itemize}

The headline metric is the OOD delivery ratio A$\to$N / N$\to$N: how many more packages the adversary-trained policy delivers than the nature-trained policy on unseen layouts, in a nature evaluation (no pursuer at test time). This is the cleanest test of OOD transfer: the adversary only exists during training, not evaluation.

\textbf{Why delivery count, not reward?} Step penalties ($-2$/step) accumulate
heavily when an agent takes long evasive paths. An agent caught early incurs fewer
step penalties than one that successfully evades but takes an inefficient path,
making average reward a poor indicator of task success. We report reward as a
secondary metric, in addition to collision count and steps.

\section{Exploratory Experiments}
\label{sec:exploratory}

Before the systematic evaluation, we ran a sequence of exploratory runs to
calibrate the task and identify which design choices mattered. We summarize the
key findings here. These runs are not used in the main statistical claims.

\textbf{Nature baseline.} The nature-trained policy achieves strong ID-A performance
(88.36 reward, 47/50 deliveries) but poor OOD transfer
($-277.16$ reward, 94/500 deliveries, 288 collisions). OOD generalization is
clearly the challenge.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \optionalpaperfigure{learning-curves.png}{0.85\linewidth}{learning-curve figure}
  \caption{Reward learning curve for the nature-trained agent during training.}
  \label{fig:learning-curves-placeholder}
  \Description{Policy learning curve showing reward trajectory.}
\end{figure}


\textbf{Deterministic vs.\ learned adversary (early runs).} Our baseline deterministic
adversary run (\codepath{outputs/default}) yielded strong adversary-trained ID-A
(98.86 A$\to$A reward, 50/50 deliveries) but poor OOD A$\to$A performance
($-75.86$, 34/500 deliveries). An early
learned adversary rerun (\codepath{outputs/learningadversary/rerun_fixed_eval})
achieved slightly higher A$\to$A ID-A reward (101.80, also 50/50 deliveries)
with similarly poor OOD A$\to$A performance ($-75.84$, 21/500 deliveries).
These single-seed exploratory runs therefore do not support a claim
that the learned adversary is harder to train against in the same-scenario
setting. They only suggest that both adversary types still suffer poor OOD
performance in this early regime. We retain both adversary types in the
systematic study below and resolve the comparison there using controlled,
multi-seed experiments.

\textbf{Distance shaping and context.} Adding distance shaping alone improved
ID-A ($A\to A = 103.98$, 50/50 deliveries) without resolving OOD falloff
($A\to A = -78.18$, 3/250 deliveries). Adding relative
package/destination context without shaping caused brittle OOD behavior
($-400$ reward, zero deliveries in some cells), consistent with state space
sparsity, an early signal of the coverage problem later confirmed in
Section~\ref{sec:audit}. The OOD numbers from experiments that combined context
features with shaping (\codepath{la_ic_ds}, \codepath{la_zs_ic_ds}) are
invalidated by the same state space explosion and are not used as evidence here.

\textbf{General-sum vs.\ zero-sum learned adversary.} Qualitative review of
rollout animations revealed that the general-sum learned adversary frequently
failed to move in ways that interfered with the delivery agent. It appeared
passive rather than actively pursuing. This motivated a switch to a strict
zero-sum objective ($r_\text{adv} = -r_\text{delivery}$), which produced
qualitatively more credible adversary behavior.
The same-scenario quantitative difference is small: general-sum
reaches 108.18 ID-A A$\to$A reward and zero-sum 107.84, with both delivering
50/50 packages and incurring zero collisions. This decision is therefore best
understood as one based on qualitative adversary behavior rather than on a
strong quantitative same-scenario advantage. The zero-sum formulation became the
reference design for all subsequent learned adversary experiments.

\section{Main Results: Deterministic Adversary Training}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:main}

We ran three protocols to establish the OOD delivery advantage. All use the baseline 5$\times$5 (or 7$\times$7) state space with
$n_\text{states} = 1{,}875$ (or $7{,}203$) and a deterministic pursuer.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiment 1: Seed Sweep (5$\times$5, 6 Seeds)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Protocol.} 5$\times$5 grid, 20k training episodes, 10 OOD layouts
$\times$ 50 eval episodes $= 500$ OOD episodes per cell, across 6 random seeds.

\midrule

\textbf{Findings.} Adversary-trained policies deliver $1.93\times$ more
packages OOD across all 6 seeds, with no reversals (ratio range: $1.52\times$--$3.39\times$).
Collisions drop 15\% (340 to 291 per 500 episodes). In the OOD adversary scenario,
the advantage is even larger: $2.31\times$ (42.8 vs.\ 18.5 deliveries per 500 eps).

A striking in-distribution failure is worth noting: in seed 456, the nature-trained
policy delivers 0/50 packages when evaluated against the adversary on its own
training layout. The adversary-trained policy handles the same layout with 50/50
deliveries. This illustrates that adversary training improves robustness even within
the training distribution.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiment 2: Large OOD Protocol (5$\times$5, 5 Seeds)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Protocol.} Same 5$\times$5 configuration, but 30 OOD layouts $\times$
100 eval episodes $= 3{,}000$ OOD episodes per cell — 6$\times$ the volume of
Experiment~1. Evaluated across 5 seeds.

\textbf{Findings.} With 3,000 OOD episodes per cell, the advantage remains stable.
Adversary-trained policies deliver $1.89\times$ more packages in the nature
OOD setting (882 vs.\ 467, mean across 5 seeds). OOD collisions drop 15\% (2,048
to 1,739 per 3,000 eps). The effect holds across all 5 seeds with no reversals.

The reward confound is visible here: in the OOD adversary scenario, the
nature-trained policy shows slightly better average reward ($-79.1$) than the
adversary-trained policy ($-84.6$) despite delivering far fewer packages (103 vs.\
191). This is because the nature-trained agent is captured quickly, ending episodes
early and accumulating fewer step penalties. This reaffirms that delivery count and not reward/score is the correct metric.

\label{sec:7x7}

\textbf{Protocol.} Scaled to 7$\times$7 (7,203 states), 40k training episodes,
15 OOD layouts $\times$ 50 eval episodes $= 750$ OOD episodes per cell, across 5 seeds.

\textbf{Findings.} The 7$\times$7 grid produces our strongest result:

over-caution persists when the pursuer is absent. The delivery count is nearly the
same, but the agent takes far more steps to accomplish it. This tradeoff is worth noting and
grows with grid complexity.

\section{Investigating the Learned Adversary}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:learned}

Our follow-up question was whether a learned (strategic, co-evolving)
adversary could produce even stronger OOD robustness. We tested five configurations
varying adversary objective, learning rate, grid size, and freeze schedule
(Experiments 4--8). Every variant appeared to show a weak OOD advantage ($\approx
1.07\times$), far below the deterministic result. But these results turned out to be entirely invalid.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Methodological Audit: Experiments 4--8 Invalidated}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:audit}

After noticing that the four 5$\times$5 learned adversary configurations (Experiments 4--6
and~8) produced bit-for-bit identical A$\to$N OOD delivery counts
(79/135/93/131 per seed, regardless of adversary objective, learning rate, or
freeze schedule), we traced the cause. Experiment~7 (7$\times$7 grid) produced
different raw counts but was found to be invalid for the same structural reason.
All five experiment configs had \codepath{include_relative_package_destination}
set to true, a feature that augments the state with the relative $(dx, dy)$ offset from
the agent to the package and a second $(dx, dy)$ offset from the agent to the
delivery destination. The intention was to introduce a metric into the state space that would aid the delivery agent in package delivery. Instead, it had severe negative effects by drastically expanding the state space.

On a 5$\times$5 grid, each delta spans $-4$ to $+4$ ($= 9$ values). The two offset
pairs together add four independent dimensions, multiplying the state space by
$9^4 = 6{,}561$:

\[
  1{,}875 \times 6{,}561 = 12{,}301{,}875 \text{ states}
\]

With 40k training episodes and $\sim$200 steps each ($\approx 8$M transitions), only
$\sim$3,500 Q-table entries were ever populated which covered just 0.02\% of the table.
Every OOD state lookup returned all zeros. The fallback action was
\codepath{random.choice(all_actions)}, seeded identically across all five experiment
variants. As a result, the identical delivery counts were really just the
same seeded random walk replayed five times and not policy comparisons.

The 1.07$\times$ ``null result'' from Experiments 4--8 was not a real measurement. All OOD results from these experiments are discarded.

\textbf{Lessons for tabular RL evaluation.} This failure mode is easy to miss since
ID-A results remain valid (training-layout states ARE populated regardless of state
space size), so the policy appears well-trained. Only OOD evaluation, where novel
state combinations appear, exposes the coverage gap. We therefore checked OOD
Q-table coverage before treating later learned adversary OOD results as valid.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiment 9: First Valid Learned Adversary Measurement}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:exp9}

removed, restoring the 1,875-state space (matching Experiments 1--3). All other
learned adversary settings were retained: zero-sum objective, adversary acts every
step, 40k episodes, 5 seeds.

\textbf{State coverage verified.} Post-run diagnostics on the delivery policy trained in the learned adversary regime
confirmed 3,355 nonzero Q-values distributed across
924 states with at least one nonzero action value. In the OOD A$\to$N evaluation,
28,775 of 45,649 state lookups (63.0\%) hit states with at least one nonzero

Experiments~4--8, so the evaluation reflects genuine policy behavior rather than
a seeded random walk.

\section{State Representation and Layout Diversity}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:stateexp}

Experiment~9 establishes a valid baseline for the learned adversary with 1,875
states (5-seed mean: 96.4 OOD N$\to$N deliveries). We then tested three design
interventions to improve OOD robustness: proximity-bucket encoding (Exps~10--11),
coarse destination direction (Exps~12--13), and multi-layout training (Exps~14--15),
with a combined variant in Exps~16--17. Each intervention is evaluated with both a
deterministic and a learned (zero-sum) adversary across 5 seeds.
The Exp~9 baseline is the full 5-seed mean of 96.4.

\subsection{State Space Reference}

To understand the results, it helps to be explicit about state space size and
coverage:
\[
  n_\text{states} = N^2 \times d_\text{slot} \times 3
\]
where $d_\text{slot} = N^2$ normally, $= 5$ for proximity encoding (Exps~10--11),
and a factor of $9^2 = 81$ is appended for coarse direction (Exps~12--13).

\textbf{Coverage intuition.} With 40k episodes and $\sim$200 steps each
($\approx 8$M transitions), a 1,875-state table gets $\approx 4{,}267$ visits per
state on average (near-saturated). A 151,875-state table gets $\approx 53$ visits
per state (sparse). A 12.3M-state table gets $\approx 0.65$ visits per state
(effectively empty). This coverage framing predicts the results below.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiments 10--11: Proximity Encoding}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{Design.} Replace the adversary's full grid position (25 values on 5$\times$5)
with a 5-bucket directional proximity code (not-nearby / N / S / E / W, radius 2).
State space shrinks from 1,875 to 375. 

\textbf{Results.} In Experiments~10--11, OOD N$\to$N falls to 48.2 in
both variants, indicating that the representation change is harmful in general.
Relative to the OOD N$\to$N baseline from Exp~9, this is roughly a
50\% regression (96.4 $\to$ 48.2). The adversary-trained ratios are
1.40$\times$ for deterministic and 1.97$\times$ for learned,
but these ratios are inflated by the collapse of the N$\to$N denominator rather
than by strong absolute performance.

Crucially, the protocols still perform poorly in absolute terms. For the
deterministic variant, OOD A$\to$N falls from 151.8 (Exp~1) to 67.6 (Exp~10).
For the learned variant, OOD A$\to$N rises slightly from 91.8 (Exp~9) to 94.8
(Exp~11), but this is still well below the OOD A$\to$N performance in experiment 1. The proximity encoding removes too much spatial structure for the
agent to learn reliable navigation. For example, whether the adversary is 2
spaces N and 1 space W, 2 spaces N and 1 space E, or 1 space N and 1 space E,
the state will just encode that information as N.

\textbf{Verdict: harmful.} Reducing spatial resolution via proximity encoding is harmful, even with a smaller state space.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiments 12--13: Coarse Destination Direction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{Design.} Add an 81-value feature encoding the agent's coarse direction toward
the package using 9 octant bins (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW, and ``here'') and
toward the delivery destination using the same 9 bins. The state space is
$1{,}875 \times 81 = 151{,}875$. Unlike raw coordinate offsets, octant direction uses a compact 81-value encoding that keeps the state space far smaller than the buggy context expansion while still providing directional signal (more so than proximity encoding).

\textbf{Results.} OOD N$\to$N improves from 96.4 to 115.8, roughly a
20\% gain over its baseline, with ID
performance maintained at $\sim$48--50/50. OOD collisions remain high
($\sim$381/500), likely because the 81$\times$ state expansion still limits
coverage density.

The representation benefit is clearest for N$\to$N and does not translate into
a stronger adversary-training effect. With a learned adversary, OOD A$\to$N
rises from 91.8 (Exp~9) to 117.2, while for the deterministic adversary OOD
A$\to$N falls from 151.8 (Exp~1) to 121.2. The net effect is convergence.
Both variants end up with near-parity A$\to$N/N$\to$N ratios
(1.05$\times$ deterministic, 1.01$\times$ learned), suggesting that the final
OOD outcome here depends more on the representation than on adversary type.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiments 14--15: Multi-Layout Training}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{Design.} Train on 10 randomly sampled layouts per run, selecting one each
episode. The state space remains 1,875 (unchanged). Rather than adding information
to the state, this approach diversifies the training curriculum.

\textbf{Results.}

\label{tab:optionD}
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Config} & \textbf{N$\to$N Del} & \textbf{A$\to$N Del} & \textbf{Ratio} & \textbf{OOD N$\to$N Coll}  \\
\midrule
Deterministic Baseline (Exp~1)   & 78.5  & 151.8  & 1.93$\times$ & $\sim$340  \\
Learned Baseline (Exp~9)   & 96.4  & 91.8  & 0.95$\times$ & $\sim$305   \\
Deterministic multi (Exp~14)          & 230.8 & 213.0 & 0.92$\times$ & $\sim$28    \\
Learned multi (Exp~15)           & 230.8 & 219.2 & 0.95$\times$ & $\sim$28    \\

OOD N$\to$N delivery more than doubles, indicating a general improvement from multi-layout training. More remarkably,
OOD collisions collapse from $\sim$300/500 to $\sim$28/500, a $>$10$\times$
reduction. The mechanism is generalizable obstacle avoidance: with 10 diverse shelf
configurations, the Q-table learns movement strategies that transfer to novel
obstacle arrangements.

The improvement holds for both training protocols in absolute terms. With a deterministic adversary OOD A$\to$N rises
from 151.8 (Exp~1) to 213.0 (Exp~14, which also had more training time), and for a learned adversary it rises from 91.8 (Exp~9) to 219.2 (Exp~15), more than double.
Nature-trained and adversary-trained agents converge on roughly the same OOD delivery level once the layout distribution is diverse enough to force generalized navigation.
This is the most practically significant finding in this section. Multi-layout training lifts absolute performance across the board, not merely for one protocol.

\textbf{Adversary training and type become far less important.} Deterministic
(213.0) and learned (219.2) multi-layout variants differ by only a few percent
in OOD A$\to$N delivery, and both show near-parity ratios. This again indicates
that under multi-layout training, state-space coverage appears to be the
dominant variable in delivery performance, with adversary-type differences
becoming much smaller than in the single-layout setting.

\textbf{ID tradeoff.} ID delivery degrades slightly from $\sim$49--50/50 to roughly
$40$--$45/50$. Agents
trained on 10 layouts spread their Q-table visits across more shelf configurations
and do not optimize as sharply for the single evaluation layout.

\textbf{Verdict: dominant OOD result.} Multi-layout training is the most effective
intervention tested, and it does so without any state space expansion.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Experiments 16--17: Coarse Direction Combined with Multi-Layout}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{Design.} Combine coarse direction from Experiments~12--13 with multi-layout training from Experiments~14--15. The state space is 151,875. The hypothesis is a potential additive benefit from both layout
diversity for obstacle avoidance and direction features for navigation.

\textbf{Results.} The OOD N$\to$N delivery mean is 183.0. This is greater than coarse direction alone (115.8), but substantially below multi-layout alone (214.8). OOD collision counts average roughly $\sim$314 in both variants, which is far above
Exps~14--15's $\sim$28 and approaching Exps~12--13's $\sim$381. The
A$\to$N/N$\to$N ratio is near one for both protocols, 0.95$\times$ (Exp~16) and
0.95$\times$ (Exp~17), consistent with Exps~14--15. In absolute terms, A$\to$N rises from 151.8 to 173.4 (Exp~16) and from 91.8 to 173.2 (Exp~17). Again, it seems that adversary type does not make a significant difference, and adversarial training in general does not meaningfully improve generalization.

\textbf{Why the combination fails.} Multi-layout training (Exps~14--15) and coarse
direction (Exps~12--13) use incompatible mechanisms. Multi-layout training
works since the state space stays small (1,875) so that
multi-layout training achieves dense coverage. Each of 10 layouts contributes
$\sim$4k episodes $\times$ 200 steps $= 800$k state-action transitions across a
1,875$\times$4 table, guaranteeing near-complete coverage. OOD layouts see states that were visited in training. Coarse direction works because of state enrichment. It
expands the table 81$\times$ to encode navigational direction. In combination, the
81$\times$ expansion reduces the coverage density that makes multi-layout training
work. OOD collision rates return to Exps~12--13 levels, confirming that the 10-layout
curriculum can no longer cover the expanded space. State enrichment and state coverage pose a tradeoff in tabular Q-learning.

\subsection{Experiments 18--19: Adversary Freeze Schedule}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:freeze}

A natural hypothesis for the learned adversary's lack of effectiveness is co-learning
non-stationarity. Because both agents update simultaneously, the delivery
agent never stabilizes against a consistent training pressure. We tested this by
freezing the learned adversary's Q-table at episode 10k, letting the delivery agent
train against a static policy for the remaining 30k episodes (Exp~18). We also
combined a freeze at episode 20k with multi-layout training (Exp~19).

Exp~18: freeze@10k (single-layout)  & 104.0  & 100.5  & 0.97$\times$ \\

\textbf{Findings.} Freezing the adversary raises the single-layout ratio from

Multi-layout + freeze (Exp~19) does not help. Relative to plain learned
multi-layout training (Exp~15), OOD A$\to$N actually falls from 219.2 to 208.8,
while N$\to$N rises slightly from 230.8 to 234.0.

\textbf{Interpretation.} Stabilizing the learned adversary with a freeze does not
recover a deterministic-style OOD advantage. Single-layout freeze remains near
parity, and multi-layout + freeze slightly underperforms plain multi-layout on
the adversary-trained policy. The dominant effect is still layout diversity, not
learned adversary stabilization.

\section{Boundary Conditions of the OOD Benefit}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{sec:boundary}

For this section, the relevant baseline is the corresponding multi-layout result. In the base single-agent,
single-package, single-shelf, 5$\times$5 setting with multi-layout training
(Exp~14), deterministic adversary training does not outperform nature training
on OOD layouts (213.0 vs.\ 230.8 deliveries, $0.92\times$).

We now probe whether modifying task scale or topology causes deterministic adversary
OOD benefit to emerge, remain near parity, or further decline under that multi-layout regime.
We ran five experiments that vary one dimension at a time: task complexity (2~packages),
cooperative multi-agent (2~delivery agents), static obstacle density (2~shelves),
adversary count (2~deterministic pursuers), and the interaction of grid size with obstacle density (7$\times$7 with 2~shelves).

All five experiments apply the same best practices established in
Section~\ref{sec:stateexp}: multi-layout training (10~training layouts),
distance shaping (scale 1.0), 40{,}000 training episodes, 10~OOD layouts,
and 5~seeds.

Two new environment parameters were implemented: \codepath{shelf_count} (multiple
non-overlapping shelf obstacles; static — not encoded in state) and
\codepath{adversary_count} (multiple deterministic pursuers; state encodes
the nearest pursuer's position, preserving the 1{,}875-state space).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{2 Packages (Experiment 20)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Adding a second package raises state space to 5{,}625 states ($3^2$ package
states). The OOD ratio is $1.81\times$, a strong benefit (5/5 seeds
positive, N$\to$N: 188.0, A$\to$N: 340.8). Adversary training substantially
improves OOD delivery on the two-package task. Relative to the multi-layout
baseline from Exp~14 ($0.92\times$), this is a clear re-emergence of an OOD
advantage under a harder task. This is consistent with the coverage-diversification mechanism still helping when the task
becomes a longer sequential pickup-and-delivery problem. It is also consistent with deterministic adversary training being most beneficial when state space coverage is naturally lower, without state space exploding, and the adversary forces the agent into more evasion scenarios.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{2 Delivery Agents (Experiment 21)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Two cooperative agents sharing a joint Q-table ($4^2 = 16$ joint actions,
62{,}500 states) produce a $0.89\times$ OOD ratio. The enlarged joint
state-action space is too large to cover well in 40{,}000 training episodes,
and relative to the Exp~14 multi-layout baseline ($0.92\times$), this is a slight further decline.
The result is therefore consistent with a training-budget limitation rather
than with any special failure of deterministic adversary training itself.
Again, the binding constraint appears to be state coverage, not layout
diversity.
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{2 Static Shelves, 5$\times$5 (Experiment 22)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Two shelves on 5$\times$5 keep the state space at 1{,}875 (shelves not encoded),
and the OOD ratio is $1.07\times$ (3/5 seeds positive, N$\to$N: 156.2, A$\to$N:
167.8). This is only a weak positive result: the mean is above 1, but the seed
pattern is mixed and the effect is modest. Relative to the Exp~14 multi-layout
baseline ($0.92\times$), this is a small positive shift rather than strong
evidence of a robust benefit. Denser static obstacles on a small grid can move deterministic adversary
training slightly above parity, but not by much. It seems the potential for a
dense layout to force more layout-specific and less generalizable evasion tactics was not fully realized.
One caveat is that seed 42 produced a degenerate in-distribution layout with
zero deliveries for all policies. OOD metrics from that seed remain valid and are
included in the reported means.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{2 Deterministic Adversaries (Experiment 23)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Doubling adversary count (nearest pursuer encoded; 1{,}875 states unchanged)
produces a $1.06\times$ ratio (3/5 seeds positive, 2/5 negative). Doubling the
pursuer count provides a slight OOD advantage for the adversarially trained policy, but not in a major or definitive way. 
The mixed seed outcomes indicate that stronger pursuit
pressure is not, by itself, enough to create a large generalization advantage
once multi-layout training is already providing broad coverage. Relative to the
Exp~14 multi-layout baseline ($0.92\times$), this is again only a small shift
into positive territory.

benefit (4/5 seeds positive, N$\to$N: 83.2, A$\to$N: 104.8). The benefit is stronger
than on the smaller grid (Exp~22: $1.07\times$ on 5$\times$5).

On the 7$\times$7 grid, adding a second shelf does not erase the OOD advantage:
the adversary-trained policy still outperforms the nature-trained policy in 4 of
5 seeds and in the mean. The comparison to Exp~22 suggests that denser
obstacles are less damaging when the grid has more navigable space, though this
is only suggestive because grid size, state space, and training setup also
differ across the two experiments. The main takeaway is simply that the
adversarial advantage emerges again in this larger, denser setting even
though its magnitude is much smaller than the single-layout 7$\times$7 baseline
from Exp~3.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Summary: The Role of Topology and Scale}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The boundary of the OOD benefit is not determined by added complexity in
general. Starting from the multi-layout baseline in Exp~14 ($0.92\times$),
some harder settings push deterministic adversary training back above parity, though for several only weakly.
The clearest exception is state-space explosion. When joint state coverage collapses, even a modest
benefit fails to emerge. Table~\ref{tab:boundary} shows four main outcomes.

\textbf{Strong emergence (Exp~20: $1.81\times$, 5/5 seeds):} Adding a second
package moves deterministic adversary training far above the multi-layout
baseline and produces the clearest renewed OOD advantage in this section.

\textbf{Continued non-benefit (Exp~21: $0.89\times$, 1/5 seeds):} Two
cooperative agents expand the state space to 62{,}500, beyond the apparent
coverage capacity of 40{,}000 training episodes. This is another case
where training budget appears to become the limiting factor.

\textbf{Moderate emergence (Exp~24: $1.26\times$, 4/5 seeds):} The 7$\times$7 grid with
2 shelves produces a meaningful OOD advantage. Relative to Exp~22, this
suggests that added obstacles are less damaging when the environment has more
space, though that comparison is not a single-factor ablation.

\textbf{Weak emergence / near-neutral (Exps~22, 23):} Two shelves on 5$\times$5
($1.07\times$) and two adversaries ($1.06\times$) both move above the Exp~14
baseline, but only slightly and inconsistently. These results are better read
as small positive shifts than as evidence of a strong positive effect.

The key variable governing failure is state space size relative to training
budget. Exp~21's 62{,}500-state joint space is the only configuration where the
deterministic adversary still fails to reach parity. Exps~22 and 24 further
suggest that obstacle density is more damaging on cramped grids than on larger
ones, but that topology claim should be read cautiously because multiple
factors change together. Overall, OOD benefits of deterministic adversary
training do re-emerge when the training task becomes more difficult in some
ways, but only when that added difficulty does not simultaneously destroy state
space coverage.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Why Does the Deterministic Adversary Help OOD?}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The deterministic pursuer forces the delivery agent to explore a wide range of
positional combinations during training on a single layout. The agent must navigate
around a pursuer that relentlessly approaches from all relative positions. This
diversifies the Q-table entries visited during training, effectively broadening the
set of positions the agent has encountered. When an OOD layout presents new shelf
configurations, the agent is more likely to have seen analogous positional
relationships.

Multi-layout training (Exps~14--15) supports part of this interpretation directly:
explicit layout diversity produces the same broad OOD improvement for both
adversary types and greatly narrows the gap between them.
Once the training curriculum covers enough positional diversity (either through an
active pursuer or through multi-layout training) without exploding the state
space, OOD robustness becomes much more likely. The adversary
in Experiments~1--3 is functioning more as a curriculum diversifier than a strategic
opponent that teaches specific evasion strategies.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Why Doesn't the Learned Adversary Help?}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The learned adversary applies a less stable training signal than the deterministic one. But this does not produce better OOD transfer in this case. The likely mechanism is co-learning non-stationarity. Because both the
delivery agent and the adversary are updating simultaneously, neither converges to
a stable policy. The delivery agent never develops consistent evasion strategies
that generalize and instead adapts to a moving target. The Q-table entries updated
in any given training phase are later overwritten as the adversary changes its
pursuit pattern. The result is a policy shaped by the specific co-evolutionary
trajectory of this training run, not a general-purpose obstacle-avoidance strategy.

This is consistent with known co-learning failure modes in two-player tabular
settings and suggests that a stable, consistent training pressure as seen with the deterministic pursuer from episode one is an important condition for the OOD
benefit observed in Experiments~1--3.

Our freeze experiments (Exps~18--19) allow us to decompose this failure. When the
learned adversary is frozen at episode 10{,}000 to halt Q-updates and lock 
epsilon to 0.05, it acts near-greedily on its current policy. The OOD ratio
rises only from $0.95\times$ (Exp~9) to $0.97\times$ (Exp~18), with OOD A$\to$N
deliveries increasing from 91.8 to 100.5 while OOD N$\to$N also rises from 96.4
to 104.0. Non-stationarity is therefore at most a minor contributor.
In this single-layout comparison, eliminating ongoing co-learning does not
create a meaningful OOD advantage and does not approach the $1.93\times$
advantage of the deterministic pursuer in Experiment~1. The remaining gap
($0.97\times$ vs.\ $1.93\times$) points more to a difference in pursuit
quality than to non-stationarity alone. The deterministic
pursuer executes a high quality, consistent chase strategy from
episode one, diversifying the delivery agent's Q-table from the very start of
training. A learned adversary, even once frozen, was shaped during early co-learning
when its own policy was weak and exploratory. The Q-table entries accumulated during
that period are qualitatively different from those accumulated under consistent,
competent pursuit.

Multi-layout training with a frozen adversary (Exp~19) confirms this interpretation with
the opposite pattern: the OOD delivery ratio falls from $0.95\times$ in Exp~15 to
$0.89\times$ in Exp~19, with A$\to$N deliveries dropping from 219.2 to 208.8. Once layout 
diversity is the
primary source of Q-table coverage, freezing the learned adversary provides no
additional help and may slightly hurt by locking in a policy that is still not a
useful training curriculum. Together, these results point to two ideal conditions for the
OOD advantage observed with the deterministic adversary: (a)~a stable adversary
policy throughout training so that Q-table updates do not cancel across phases, and
(b)~a high-quality pursuit strategy from the first episode so that the delivery agent
accumulates useful, generalizable positional experience from the outset. 

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{State Coverage as the Binding Constraint}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

The most general lesson from our experiments is that OOD robustness in tabular
Q-learning is a coverage problem. The policy can only do better than random in OOD
states that it has encountered (or that share Q-table entries with encountered states).
Design choices that increase useful coverage within a sufficiently informative
state representation often improve OOD performance. Conversely, choices that
expand the state space beyond what the training budget can cover tend to hurt OOD
performance, even when the added feature is intuitively helpful for navigation
(as Exps~16--17 demonstrate).

The practical implication of this is that for deployment in environments with diverse
layouts, multi-layout training with a small, fully-coverable state space (Exps~14--15)
is more effective than trying to encode extra information about the environment
(Exps~12--13). The two are in tension, and coverage often seems more important. Exps~10--11 show there is a limit, though, because they remove too much information about adversary location in an effort to decrease state space.

The boundary condition experiments (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}) deepen this picture.
For added packages (Exp~20: $1.81\times$), added
shelves (Exp~22: $1.07\times$; Exp~24: $1.26\times$), and added adversaries (Exp~23:
$1.06\times$) deterministic adversary training moves back above the multi-layout
baseline from Exp~14 ($0.92\times$), though only Exp~20 and Exp~24 do so by a
clearly meaningful margin. Exp~21 (two cooperative agents) instead remains below
parity at $0.89\times$, where the 62{,}500-state joint space appears to exceed
coverage capacity regardless of adversary type. Grid size also affects the strength of
the benefit under added obstacles. The 7$\times$7 grid with 2 shelves yields
$1.26\times$ versus $1.07\times$ on the 5$\times$5 grid with the same obstacle
count, consistent with the idea that the larger grid provides more navigable
space for adversary-induced positional diversity, though isn't a clean one-factor comparison.
The results point to the fact that adversarial training could provide a meaningful OOD
benefit in more complicated environments as long as state space is kept in check.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{No formal statistical tests.} We report effect sizes and seed counts and
not p-values or confidence intervals. Some of the major results (deterministic
adversary OOD advantage, multi-layout gain) involve large enough effects and several
seeds such that the directional finding is robust. The moderate-effect results
have less statistical support.

\textbf{Partial Q-table validity in Exps~1--3.} With 33--51\% nonzero hit rates
on OOD state lookups, some OOD states fall back to random actions. The directional
finding (A$\to$N $>$ N$\to$N) is robust to this noise (consistent across all
seeds), but absolute delivery counts are underestimates of the true policy's
capability.

\textbf{Tabular Q-learning only.} The state coverage mechanism is specific to
tabular methods. Function approximation (e.g., neural network Q-learning) generalizes
across states by design, which would change the dynamic of both the OOD robustness
benefit and the state space expansion tradeoffs.

\textbf{Adversary comparison is not a pure predictability-only ablation.} The
deterministic pursuer and learned adversary differ not only in predictability,
but also in policy quality, optimization stability, and whether the opponent is
co-evolving during training. The results therefore support an empirical claim
about which adversary design transfers better in this tabular setting, but do
not isolate predictability as the only causal factor.

\textbf{Single task and layout family.} All experiments use the same pickup-and-delivery
task and the same layout generation procedure. Results may not generalize to
warehouse environments with qualitatively different structure (e.g., long corridors,
multi-floor navigation, multi-agent coordination).

\textbf{OOD layouts come from one generator family.} The out-of-distribution
evaluations use unseen layouts, but those layouts are still sampled from the
same procedural generation family used throughout the project. The paper
therefore demonstrates robustness to unseen layouts within that family rather
than to arbitrary warehouse topologies or broader real-world domain shift.

\textbf{Limited training budget.} The majority of our experiments trained for 40k episodes. Considering that state space coverage was the most crucial factor in policy success, additional experiments with larger budgets should be conducted.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

We set out to test whether adversarial Q-learning produces better OOD
generalization in a gridworld warehouse. The answer is that it depends on what
kind of adversary and on the training regime.

When training on a single layout against a deterministic pursuer, OOD delivery is consistently improved
by $1.89\times$--$3.01\times$ across 16 seeded runs and two grid sizes, with no reversals.
The advantage is larger on the 7$\times$7 protocol than on the 5$\times$5
protocols (1.89$\times$--1.93$\times$ at 5$\times$5, 3.01$\times$ at 7$\times$7),
though those experiments also differ in training budget and OOD evaluation volume.
It manifests in both delivery count and collision avoidance. This is
the paper's strongest and most reliable result.

Training against a learned adversary provides little to no OOD benefit, and at times a mild
disadvantage (0.95$\times$ in the first valid single-layout measurement, Exp~9). The
paper's audit section on experiments 4--8 demonstrates how tabular RL OOD evaluations
can silently degrade into random-walk artifacts when state space size exceeds
training coverage.

The underlying mechanism is strongly tied to state coverage, especially
under the multi-layout regime, rather than to adversary type alone. Multi-layout
training with a compact state encoding (Exps~14--15) doubles OOD delivery independently
of adversary choice and collapses OOD collision rates from $\sim$300/500 to
$\sim$28/500. Enriching the state representation while simultaneously using
multi-layout training (Exps~16--17) backfires: the two mechanisms are incompatible
because one requires small state space (aliasing) and the other expands it
(enrichment). With multi-layout training, adversary type becomes much less
important than in the single-layout experiments, and adversarial training is no
longer clearly beneficial on its own (Exp~14--15). When varying layout, the
stochastic "nature" obstacle can fill out the state space comparably well to the
deterministic and learned adversaries.

The OOD benefit has context-dependent boundary conditions. Five complexity
scaling experiments (Exps~20--24, all using multi-layout training and 5~seeds) show mixed results
relative to the multi-layout deterministic baseline in Exp~14 ($0.92\times$).
Added packages produce a strong re-emergence of
benefit ($1.81\times$). A larger grid with added shelves produces a moderate
benefit ($1.26\times$ on 7$\times$7). Added shelves on 5$\times$5 and doubled
adversary count produce only weak positive shifts ($1.07\times$ and
$1.06\times$). Cooperative multi-agent settings remain below parity
($0.89\times$), where the 62{,}500-state joint Q-table appears to exceed
training coverage capacity. The critical variable is state space size relative
to training budget, not added complexity in general.

If deployment will involve diverse warehouse layouts, multi-layout
training with a minimal state encoding is more effective than adversarial training
alone. If layout diversity in training is limited, a deterministic adversary can
still provide reliable OOD robustness under the simpler single-layout conditions
of Experiments~1--3, and in some harder multi-layout settings it can again become
helpful. In both cases, a learned co-evolving adversary appears to be a nonfactor
or counterproductive with a tabular Q-learning scheme.
